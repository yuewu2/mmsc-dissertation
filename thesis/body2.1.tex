\section{Adaptive Finite Element Methods}
\label{sec:background-afem}

Let \(U\) and \(V\) be reflexive Banach spaces with dual spaces \(U^\ast\) and \(V^\ast\), respectively. Let \(\mathcal{A}:U\rightarrow V^\ast\) denote the weak residual operator associated with the problem.
For example, consider the Poisson problem
\[
-\Delta u=f \quad \text{in }\Omega,
\qquad
u=0 \quad \text{on }\partial\Omega.
\]
With \(U=V=H_0^1(\Omega)\), its weak formulation is
\[
\int_\Omega \nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x
-\int_\Omega f v\,\mathrm{d}x=0
\qquad\text{for all }v\in H_0^1(\Omega).
\]
In this case, the residual operator is
\[
\mathcal{A}(u)(v)
=
\int_\Omega \nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x
-\int_\Omega f v\,\mathrm{d}x.
\]
The continuous problem can therefore be written abstractly as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}(u)(v)=0
  \qquad\text{for all }v\in V.
  \label{eq:abstract-primal}
\end{equation}
For finite-dimensional spaces \(U_h\subset U\) and \(V_h\subset V\), a Galerkin approximation \(u_h\in U_h\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}(u_h)(v_h)=0
  \qquad\text{for all }v_h\in V_h.
  \label{eq:abstract-discrete-primal}
\end{equation}

The accuracy of \(u_h\) depends on how well the finite-dimensional spaces resolve the solution. Adaptive finite element methods (AFEM), building on the pioneering work of Babuška and Rheinboldt \cite{pioneer}, improve the discretisation iteratively using a posteriori error information. In practice, this process is commonly organised as a \textsc{Solve--Estimate--Mark--Refine} loop \cite{carstensen2014axioms}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textsc{Solve} the differential equation on the current space--time discretisation.
  \item \textsc{Estimate} a global estimator for the error quantity under consideration.
  \item \textsc{Mark} the elements by localising the error estimator.
  \item \textsc{Refine} the marked elements using a specified refinement strategy.
\end{enumerate}

The automated goal-oriented solver considered in this dissertation follows this AFEM framework. The dual-weighted residual method, introduced in the following section, provides the goal-oriented error estimator used in the \textsc{Estimate} step. The key contribution of this thesis, described in \cref{sec:background-localisation}, is an algorithm for decomposing this global estimator into local contributions for use in the \textsc{Mark} step, in combination with the standard D\"orfler strategy. The refinement strategy is also standard and is specified at the end of this chapter.

The DWR method requires an adjoint problem determined by the chosen functional \(J\). With primes denoting Fr\'echet derivatives, the continuous adjoint is: find \(z\in V\) such that
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}'(u)(w,z)=J'(u)(w)
  \qquad\text{for all }w\in U,
  \label{eq:nonlinear-adjoint}
\end{equation}
where \(u\) solves \eqref{eq:abstract-primal}. The corresponding discrete adjoint is: find \(z_h\in V_h\) such that
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}'(u_h)(w_h,z_h)=J'(u_h)(w_h)
  \qquad\text{for all }w_h\in U_h,
  \label{eq:discrete-nonlinear-adjoint}
\end{equation}
where \(u_h\) solves \eqref{eq:abstract-discrete-primal}.


\subsection{An Error Identity}
\label{subsec:nonlinear-dwr}

The following theorem is the core of the DWR method.

\begin{theorem}[Nonlinear DWR identity \cite{Becker_Rannacher_2001,ENDTMAYER202419}]
\label{thm:becker-rannacher}
Assume that \(\mathcal{A}\in C^3(U,V^\ast)\) and \(J\in C^3(U,\mathbb{R})\). For any approximation \(\widetilde{x}=(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{z})\in U\times V\),
\begin{align}
  J(u)-J(\widetilde{u})
  =
  \frac{1}{2}\rho(\widetilde{u})(z-\widetilde{z})
  +
  \frac{1}{2}
  \rho^\ast(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{z})
  (u-\widetilde{u})
  +
  \rho(\widetilde{u})(\widetilde{z})
  +
  \mathcal{R}^{(3)},
  \label{eq:nonlinear-dwr-identity}
\end{align}
where the forward and adjoint residuals are
\begin{align}
  \rho(\widetilde{u})(\cdot)
  &\coloneqq
  -\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{u})(\cdot),
  \label{eq:nonlinear-primal-residual}\\
  \rho^\ast(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{z})(\cdot)
  &\coloneqq
  J'(\widetilde{u})(\cdot)
  -
  \mathcal{A}'(\widetilde{u})
  (\cdot,\widetilde{z}).
  \label{eq:nonlinear-adjoint-residual}
\end{align}
Moreover, with \(e=u-\widetilde u\) and \(e^\ast=z-\widetilde z\), the remainder term in the error identity is cubic:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal R^{(3)}
 =\frac12\int_0^1\!\Bigl[{}
 &J'''(\widetilde u+se)(e,e,e) -\mathcal A'''(\widetilde u+se)
   (e,e,e,\widetilde z+se^\ast)\\
 &-3\mathcal A''(\widetilde u+se)(e,e,e^\ast)
 \Bigr]s(s-1)\,\mathrm ds .
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:nonlinear-dwr-remainder}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This expresses the error in the goal functional as the average of the primal and adjoint residuals evaluated at the adjoint and primal errors respectively, up to $\rho(\widetilde{u})(\widetilde{z})$ (which is zero if the discrete problems are solved exactly, and otherwise measures errors from inexact algebraic solvers), and a cubic remainder term.

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Set \(X=U\times V\), \(x=(u,z)\), \(\widetilde x=(\widetilde u,\widetilde z)\), and introduce the Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L(\widehat u,\widehat z)
  \coloneqq J(\widehat u)-\mathcal A(\widehat u)(\widehat z).
  \label{eq:dwr-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
Along the segment \(x_s=\widetilde x+s(x-\widetilde x)\), the fundamental theorem of calculus and the trapezoidal identity give
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L(x)-\mathcal L(\widetilde x)
 ={}&\frac12\bigl[\mathcal L'(x)(x-\widetilde x)
       +\mathcal L'(\widetilde x)(x-\widetilde x)\bigr] +\frac12\int_0^1
   \mathcal L'''(x_s)(x-\widetilde x)^3s(s-1)\,\mathrm ds.
 \label{eq:dwr-trapezoidal-proof}
\end{align}
The exact primal and adjoint equations make \(x\) a stationary point of \(\mathcal L\), so \(\mathcal L'(x)=0\). Expanding \(\mathcal L'(\widetilde x)\) in its primal and dual directions produces the primal residual \(\rho\) and adjoint residual \(\rho^\ast\); substituting the definition of \(\mathcal L\) gives the Galerkin correction in \eqref{eq:nonlinear-dwr-identity}. Expanding the third derivative gives \eqref{eq:nonlinear-dwr-remainder}. Complete algebraic details are given in, for example, \cite{Rannacher_2013,93,ENDTMAYER202419}.
\end{proof}

As previously mentioned, the term \(\rho(\widetilde u)(\widetilde z)\) vanishes by Galerkin orthogonality when the discrete problems are solved exactly. Otherwise it can be used to balance 
% e.g.~discretisation and errors in iterative solvers (Newton and/or Krylov iterations) \cite{Rannacher_2013}.
discretisation errors against iterative-solver errors, such as errors from Newton or Krylov iterations \cite{Rannacher_2013}.

If \(\mathcal A\) is affine and \(J\) is linear, then \(\mathcal R^{(3)}=0\), and the two half-residual terms coincide. To state the resulting linear identity explicitly, let \(a:U\times V\rightarrow\mathbb R\) be bilinear, \(\ell\in V^\ast\), and define
\begin{align}
 a(u,v)&=\ell(v) &&\forall v\in V,
 \label{eq:linear-primal}\\
 a(u_h,v_h)&=\ell(v_h) &&\forall v_h\in V_h,
 \label{eq:linear-discrete-primal}\\
 a(w,z)&=J(w) &&\forall w\in U,
 \label{eq:linear-adjoint}\\
 \rho(u_h)(v)&\coloneqq\ell(v)-a(u_h,v).
 \label{eq:linear-residual}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Exact linear goal-error identity]
\label{prop:linear-dwr}
Let \(u\in U\) solve \eqref{eq:linear-primal} and \(u_h\in U_h\) solve \eqref{eq:linear-discrete-primal}, with dual solution \(z\) defined by \eqref{eq:linear-adjoint}. Then for every \(\varphi_h\in V_h\),
\begin{equation}
  J(u)-J(u_h)
  =
  \rho(u_h)(z-\varphi_h).
  \label{eq:linear-dwr-exact}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
% \begin{proof}
% Writing \(e=u-u_h\), the primal and adjoint equations give \(\rho(u_h)(z)=a(e,z)=J(e)=J(u)-J(u_h)\). Galerkin orthogonality gives \(\rho(u_h)(\varphi_h)=0\), and subtracting this zero term proves \eqref{eq:linear-dwr-exact}.
% \end{proof}
The residual measures the defect of \(u_h\) in the continuous variational problem. The factor \(z-\varphi_h\) is the part of the QoI sensitivity that is not represented in the discrete test space.


\subsection{Computable Estimators and Effectivity}
\label{sec:background-effectivity}

Both identities above involve the unknown exact primal and adjoint solutions and are uncomputable. In practice these unknown solutions are replaced by approximations in enriched spaces, obtained, for example, by uniform \(h\)-refinement, \(p\)-enrichment, or a higher-order time approximation \cite{Becker_Rannacher_2001,ENDTMAYER202419}.

To formalise this replacement, let \(U_h\subset U_h^+\subset U\) and \(V_h\subset V_h^+\subset V\), where \(U_h^+\) and \(V_h^+\) denote enriched approximation spaces. The enriched primal and adjoint problems are
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{A}(u_h^+)(v_h^+)&=0
  &&\text{for all }v_h^+\in V_h^+,
  \label{eq:enriched-primal}\\
  \mathcal{A}'(u_h^+)(w_h^+,z_h^+)
  &=J'(u_h^+)(w_h^+)
  &&\text{for all }w_h^+\in U_h^+.
  \label{eq:enriched-adjoint}
\end{align}

The following enriched-space error identity makes precise how the resulting estimator differs from the exact goal error \cite[Theorem~2]{ENDTMAYER202419}.
\begin{theorem}[Enriched-space error representation]
\label{thm:enriched-space-dwr}
Suppose that the restrictions \(\mathcal A^+\) and \(J_h^+\) are three-times continuously differentiable on the enriched spaces and agree there with \(\mathcal A\), \(\mathcal A'\), \(J\), and \(J'\); explicitly, for \(v,w\in U_h^+\) and \(\phi\in V_h^+\),
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal A(v)(\phi)
 &=\mathcal A^+(v)(\phi),
 &
 \mathcal A'(v)(w,\phi)
 &=(\mathcal A^+)'(v)(w,\phi),\\
 J(v)&=J_h^+(v),
 &
 J'(v)(w)&=(J_h^+)'(v)(w).
\end{align*}
If \(u_h^+\) and \(z_h^+\) solve \eqref{eq:enriched-primal}--\eqref{eq:enriched-adjoint}, then for every fixed \((\widetilde u,\widetilde z)\in U_h^+\times V_h^+\),
\begin{align}
 J(u)-J(\widetilde u)
 ={}&J(u)-J(u_h^+)+\eta^+,
 \label{eq:enriched-exact-splitting}\\
 \eta^+
 \coloneqq{}&\tfrac12\rho(\widetilde u)(z_h^+-\widetilde z)
 +\tfrac12\rho^\ast(\widetilde u,\widetilde z)
  (u_h^+-\widetilde u)
 +\rho(\widetilde u)(\widetilde z)
 +\mathcal R_+^{(3)}.
 \label{eq:enriched-nonlinear-dwr}
\end{align}
Here \(\mathcal R_+^{(3)}\) is given by \eqref{eq:nonlinear-dwr-remainder} with the exact errors replaced by their enriched-space counterparts.
\end{theorem}

\cref{eq:enriched-exact-splitting} shows that \(\eta^+\) differs from the goal error by the remaining error of the enriched primal solution \(J(u)-J(u_h^+)\). Therefore, \(\eta^+\) can approximate the goal error only when this remaining term is sufficiently small. This situation is captured by a \emph{saturation assumption} \cite{saturation}
\begin{equation}
|J(u)-J(u_h^+)|
\le b_h |J(u)-J(\widetilde u)|,
\qquad 0<b_h<1,
\label{eq:goal-saturation}
\end{equation}
which in general is very difficult to prove.
Combining \eqref{eq:enriched-exact-splitting} and \eqref{eq:goal-saturation} with the triangle inequality yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{1+b_h}|\eta^+|
\le |J(u)-J(\widetilde u)|
\le \frac{1}{1-b_h}|\eta^+|.
\label{eq:enriched-reliability-efficiency}
\end{equation}
Hence, under saturation, the computable estimator \(\eta^+\) bounds the unknown goal error from both sides \cite{reli_eff}. The upper bound gives reliability, since the estimator cannot substantially underestimate the error. The lower bound gives efficiency, since it cannot substantially overestimate it.

% \yue{omit the iteration term now}
In the computations, we always neglect both the cubic remainder and the iteration error in \eqref{eq:enriched-nonlinear-dwr}. \(\mathcal R_+^{(3)}\) is of higher order with respect to the primal and adjoint errors. The algebraic correction  \(\rho(\tilde{u})(\tilde{z})\), on the other hand, represents the error due to the inexact solution of the discrete primal problem. We therefore solve each discrete problem to a sufficiently small tolerance such that this term is negligible. Under these approximations, we obtain the practical symmetric nonlinear estimator
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{global}}
\coloneqq
\frac12\rho(u_h)(z_h^+-z_h)
+
\frac12\rho^\ast(u_h,z_h)(u_h^+-u_h),
% -
% \rho(u_h)(z_h),
\label{eq:practical-nonlinear-estimator}
\end{equation}
where \(u_h\) and \(z_h\) are the primal and adjoint solutions on the current discretisation, while \(u_h^+\) and \(z_h^+\) are their enriched counterparts.

If \(\mathcal A\) is affine and \(J\) is linear, the estimator can be reduced to
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{global}}
\coloneqq
\rho(u_h)(z^\star),
\label{eq:computable-dwr-two-solves}
\end{equation}
where we use the dual weight
\begin{equation}
z^\star=z_h^+-\Pi_{h}z_h^+
\label{eq:computable-dwr-interpolated-weight}
\end{equation}
as a less expensive alternative. Here, \(z_h^+\in V_h^+\) is the enriched adjoint solution, and \(\Pi_{h}:V_{h}^{+}\rightarrow V_{h}\) denotes the interpolation from the enriched space--time space into the primal space. The weight \(z^\star\) avoids an additional low-order adjoint solve.

Finally, when an independently computed reference value for \(J(u)\) is available, the accuracy of the global estimator can be assessed using the \emph{effectivity index}
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{eff}}
\coloneqq
\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{global}}}{J(u)-J(u_h)}.
\label{eq:signed-effectivity}
\end{equation}
An effectivity index close to one indicates good agreement between the estimator and the true goal error. Extensive numerical experience with DWR suggests that polynomial-degree enrichment typically yields effectivity indices close to one \cite{ENDTMAYER202419}.
