\section{Motivation and Background}
\label{sec:introduction-background}

In many partial differential equation (PDE) problems, the numerical solution is not required with uniform accuracy throughout the computational domain. What matters instead is the accurate evaluation of a specific \emph{quantity of interest} (QoI), a functional  
\begin{equation}
  J:U\longrightarrow\mathbb R
  \label{eq:intro-qoi-map}
\end{equation}
that encodes why the simulation is being run in the first place, where $U$ is the state space. If $u\in U$ is the exact solution and $u_h$ is a finite element approximation, the error relevant to the stated purpose of the computation is
\begin{equation}
  e_J\coloneqq J(u)-J(u_h).
  \label{eq:intro-goal-error}
\end{equation}
For an evolution problem, $J$ may describe a terminal observation, an accumulated response over time, or a combination of the two, and it may be linear or nonlinear in the state.

Conventional adaptive finite element methods (AFEM) commonly seek to control a norm of the state error $u-u_h$ by a \textsc{Solve--Estimate--Mark--Refine} cycle \cite{pioneer,Ainsworth_Oden_1997,Eriksson_Estep_Hansbo_Johnson_1995,MorinNochettoSiebert2002,carstensen2014axioms}. This is appropriate when the accuracy of the state throughout the computational domain is itself the objective. However, when the aim is to compute only a selected output, the relevant question is not where the numerical error is largest, but where it has the largest effect on that output. Refinement based only on a global state norm may therefore spend computational effort in regions that have little influence on $J$. Goal-oriented adaptivity instead seeks to identify and reduce the discretisation errors that matter for the chosen functional.

The dual-weighted residual (DWR) method provides a systematic framework for goal-oriented error estimation \cite{Becker_Rannacher_2001,Bangerth_Rannacher_2003}. It combines the residual of the discrete primal solution with a solution of an adjoint problem. The residual measures the defect of the numerical state in the variational equation, while the adjoint measures the sensitivity of the QoI to that defect. Because the adjoint is determined by $J$, changing the functional while keeping the primal PDE fixed can change the dual weight, the local refinement indicators, and ultimately the adapted mesh.

Theoretical analyses of goal-oriented error estimation have established guaranteed computable bounds and convergence results, while more abstract analyses have investigated the optimality of goal-oriented adaptive algorithms \cite{Ainsworth_Rankin_2012,Ladevese_Pled_Chamoin_2013,Mommer_Stevenson_2009,Feischl_Praetorius_van_der_Zee_2016}. Although the present dissertation focuses on a single quantity of interest, the goal-oriented framework has also been extended to multiple quantities of interest \cite{multi1,multi2}.

The use of adjoint equations and duality for estimating errors in selected functionals is broader than the DWR framework alone. Giles and S\"uli provide a broad overview of adjoint methods in a posteriori finite element error analysis for quantities of interest, and the post-processing of integral functionals to obtain more accurate, potentially superconvergent, approximations \cite{Giles_Suli_2002}. Pierce and Giles further distinguish between adjoint methods, which estimate or correct the error in a particular output functional, and defect methods, which aim to improve the accuracy of the entire numerical solution \cite{Pierce_Giles_2004}. In an engineering setting, Prudhomme and Oden applied goal-oriented error estimation to control pointwise errors for elliptic problems by solving an associated dual problem and evaluating residual-based bounds \cite{PRUDHOMME_1999}. These studies illustrate how adjoint-based error estimation can be adapted to different error sources, physical models, and application-specific outputs.

The present dissertation adopts the DWR framework because it is a broad and useful abstraction that applies to many problems, and hence is amenable to automation. 
Exact DWR identities contain errors in the primal and adjoint solutions that are not available computationally. In practice, these terms are approximated using enriched finite element spaces. The detailed identities, computable approximations, and the assumptions under which they estimate the true goal error are presented in \cref{chap:background}.


\section{The Localisation Problem in Space--Time}
\label{sec:introduction-localisation}

A global DWR estimator indicates whether the selected output is evaluated to sufficient accuracy, but it does not by itself determine where the discretisation should be refined to improve it if it is inadequate. To drive adaptation, it must be decomposed into contributions associated with spatial cells and time intervals, to decide where and when to focus computation effort.

In standard practice this localisation is achieved by integration by parts, and the analysis is done by hand on paper. This integration by parts produces strong cell residuals, spatial flux jumps, boundary terms, and, for discontinuous time discretisations, temporal-jump terms. These contributions are interpretable for a fixed equation, but their derivation depends on the differential operator, boundary conditions, and discretisation. The necessity of repeating that manual derivation for every new variational problem limits the use of DWR only to experts.

This problem has been considered before. Variational filtering and partition-of-unity localisation avoid an explicit strong-form derivation by acting directly on the weak residual \cite{BraackErn2003,Richter_Wick_2015}. Space--time partition-of-unity DWR methods have been developed for several classes of nonstationary problems \cite{ThieleWick2024,R6,non-flow}. Their local contributions are naturally associated with basis functions or patches. 
Beyond these generic constructions, localisation can also exploit structure specific to a problem class or discretisation. Examples include gradient-averaged and nodal decompositions for convection--diffusion transport \cite{KUZMIN20101674}, equilibrated flux and potential reconstructions for elliptic and parabolic approximations \cite{MOZOLEVSKI2015127,CREUSE2023323}, space--time error splitting for dynamically changing meshes \cite{Schmich_Vexler_2008}, and coarse-neighbourhood indicators for high-contrast multiscale models \cite{CHUNG2016625}.
%\pef{Can we say something about why we don't follow any of these approaches?}

In this dissertation, the choice of localisation is guided by three requirements. First, the localisation should be suitable for automation. It should not require a PDE-specific reconstruction. Second, it should provide informative contributions with a interpretation. In particular, we wish to clearly distinguish error contributions supported on different space-time mesh entities. Finally, the same decomposition should apply to discontinuous-Galerkin time discretisation, nonmatching spatial meshes, and the global DWR estimator.

Partition-of-unity localisation is particularly attractive from the first perspective because it acts directly on the weak residual and is well suited to variational automation. It distributes the global DWR estimator through locally supported functions \(\{\chi_i\}_{i=1}^{M}\) satisfying \(\sum_i\chi_i=1\). For example, in the constructions considered in \cite{Richter_Wick_2015,ThieleWick2024,R6}, the resulting quantities are naturally associated with nodes, degrees of freedom, or temporal elements. These PU contributions have a valid variational interpretation, but they are not tied to specific space-time mesh entities. In particular, since a single PU function may overlap several spatial elements and temporal interfaces, its contribution may combine different components of the residual. An additional redistribution or marking rule is therefore needed to convert the PU contributions into indicators for specific space--time mesh entities.
The remaining approaches exploit additional structure that is not assumed in the present framework. For example, space--time error-splitting techniques are valuable for balancing spatial and temporal effects, but their primary purpose is to separate error sources rather than to recover the geometric residual entities themselves. Coarse-neighbourhood indicators are aimed at high-contrast multiscale problems and rely on coefficient structures that are not present in all of the model problems considered here.

For these reasons, this dissertation develops an alternative strategy. 
The localisation developed in this dissertation is motivated by the stationary \emph{bubble--cone} construction of Rognes and Logg \cite{Rognes_Logg_2013}. That construction recovers polynomial approximations to the cell and facet residual densities by evaluating a weak residual on so-called bubble and cone test functions, supported on a single cell or facet respectively. Here the same principle is extended to space--time slabs. Tensor-product spatial and temporal test functions are used in a hierarchical sequence to recover volume, spatial-facet, temporal-face, and, when nonzero, mixed-ridge contributions. At each stage, contributions already exposed on higher-dimensional entities are subtracted before the next residual density is recovered. The resulting entity contributions are paired with a computable DWR weight to obtain signed cell--slab indicators.

In this dissertation, time is divided into slabs and discretised by discontinuous Galerkin methods, which generalise the familiar implicit Euler discretisation\footnote{In particular, at lowest order and with a certain quadrature approximation to the integrals, it recovers implicit Euler exactly.}. The variational statement of the problem solved at each timestep contains both slab-interior actions and jump terms at temporal interfaces. This is very advantageous in the context of adaptivity because it allows the use different spatial meshes at different times (imagine e.g.\ a wave propagating from one side of the domain to another). However, the cost of this flexibility is that the state at the previous timestep must be transferred forward between meshes, and adjoint sensitivity must be propagated backwards using the adjoint of the same discrete transfer. The transferred interface defect is also part of the temporal residual and must be treated consistently by the localisation.

Time dependence also introduces a choice in the mass or time-derivative pairing. For a standard parabolic equation the pairing is typically
\begin{equation}
  m_0(w,v)=(w,v)_\Omega,
  \label{eq:intro-l2-mass-pairing}
\end{equation}
so the dG jump term has the form $m_0([U],V^+) = ([U],V^+)_\Omega$. In contrast, a $H^1$-based evolution problem may use
\begin{equation}
  m_1(w,v)=(w,v)_\Omega+\alpha(\nabla w,\nabla v)_\Omega,
  \qquad \alpha>0.
  \label{eq:intro-h1-mass-pairing}
\end{equation}
The jump term then contains $\alpha(\nabla[U],\nabla V^+)_\Omega$. When this term is localised elementwise, spatial integration by parts produces contributions on spatial facets at the temporal interface. Their support is the intersection of a spatial facet and a temporal face, which is the mixed space--time ridge stated above.

% For the standard spatial $L^2$ pairing, the discontinuous-Galerkin jump term acts on a temporal face. If the pairing contains spatial derivatives, integration over that face can additionally reveal a contribution on the intersection of a spatial facet and a temporal interface. This lower-dimensional entity is referred to here as a mixed space--time ridge. It does not arise for every problem, but when it does it is crucial to account for it.

We numerically assess the quality of our error estimation and space--time localisation in two ways.
The \emph{effectivity index} compares the global estimator with the true goal error, where it is available. This tests the quality of the global DWR estimate. The \emph{localisation gap} measures by how much the recovered local contributions exceed the global DWR estimate. This tests the quality of the localisation.

\section{Objectives and Main Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction-contributions}

The objective of this dissertation is to develop and investigate an automated, goal-oriented, space--time adaptive finite element method whose local indicators are constructed from the weak variational problem rather than from a manual and problem-specific strong-residual derivation. 

The bubble--cone residual recovery is extended from stationary finite element problems to discontinuous-Galerkin time discretisations. The construction distinguishes volume, spatial-facet, and temporal-face residual entities and combines them into signed cell--slab DWR contributions.


\section{Code and Software}
\label{sec:introduction-code}

The implementation is written in Python using \texttt{Firedrake}, which represents and assembles finite element variational forms through the Unified Form Language (UFL) \cite{Firedrake}. UFL differentiation is used to form the linearised and transposed spatial operators and the derivatives of the specified goal functional. The time discretisation follows the automated Galerkin time-stepping framework developed in and around \texttt{Irksome} \cite{Farrell_Kirby_Marchena_2021,andrews2026automatedgalerkintimestepping}.

The development repository can be found at https://github.com/yuewu2/mmsc-dissertation.



\section{Structure of the Dissertation}
\label{sec:introduction-structure}

\Cref{chap:background} develops the mathematical and computational framework. It first introduces AFEM and the linear and nonlinear DWR error representations, then describes how they may be computed in practice with enriched spaces. It next reviews residual localisation and develops the stationary and space--time bubble--cone recovery, including temporal interfaces, the optional mixed ridge, temporal quadrature, and localisation diagnostics. The chapter concludes by incorporating these components into a complete slabwise adaptive algorithm including transfer between nonmatching spatial meshes.

\Cref{chap:experiments} presents the numerical investigation. The rotating-hill heat equation compares automated bubble recovery with a manually derived strong-residual strategy. The advection--diffusion example demonstrates the dependence of the adapted space--time mesh on the chosen goal functional. The nonlinear BBM example then tests the estimator for a dispersive equation and uses a localisation gap to identify the mixed space--time ridge. The final L-shaped parabolic \(p\)-Laplace example examines the nonlinear estimator in the presence of a re-entrant-corner singularity and a localised observation region.

The final \Cref{chap:conclusion} summarises the findings, limitations, and directions for further work.
